He describes a specific, involuntary habit: counting the seconds between hearing a distant explosion and its impact. In a combat zone, this skill was vital for survival. It helped soldiers determine if incoming mortar fire was targeting their location or landing elsewhere. If the sound traveled far before hitting the ground, it meant the danger was not immediate. This rapid calculation allowed them to stay alert and safe while moving between daily activities like sleeping and eating. ### Why This Matters McDowell’s story highlights how PTSD affects the brain’s threat detection system long after a war ends. The brain learns to associate loud noises with life-or-death situations. Even in a safe environment, such as his home in the country, his brain automatically reactivates this survival mechanism.
